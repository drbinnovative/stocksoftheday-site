Do stocks recover after a 25% one-day crash? We checked 1,648 of them.

Every currently listed US stock that fell 25% or more in a single trading day from December 2015 to November 2026, and whether it ever got back to the price it closed at the day before the crash.

"Back" means the stock closed at or above its pre-crash price at least once in that period.

The short answer

Most don't, at least not quickly. Fewer than half of these stocks got back to their pre-crash price within six months, and after a full year, 44% still hadn't. The ones that did make it back typically took about 51 trading days, roughly two and a half months.

A drop of half or more usually means something fundamental broke: a failed drug trial, an accounting problem, a business that lost its biggest customer. Only about 1 in 5 of those got back within a year.

In March 2020, hundreds of stocks crashed together in the COVID sell-off, and the whole market came roaring back within months. Nearly 4 in 5 of that year's crashes recovered. In a normal year, when a stock crashes on its own news, it's closer to 1 in 3.

What about buying right after the crash?

Measured from the closing price on the crash day instead, six months later the stocks were higher about half the time (49.5%), and the typical (median) result was a 0.6% loss. A few big winners pull the average up, but for the typical stock, buying the day of the crash was close to a coin flip.

A recent example

Himax (HIMX), November 5, 2026: closed at $10.44, then fell 26% to $7.68 in one day. It bounced 10.5% the next day, but at $8.49 it was still about 19% below where it started. A one-day bounce isn't a recovery. This study measures against the price before the crash.

How we did it

  • Currently listed US stocks from December 2015 to November 6, 2026 (1,648 crashes across 1,135 stocks). Daily prices from Tiingo.
  • A crash is a close at least 25% below the previous day's close.
  • Only stocks priced at $5 or more and trading at least $5 million a day on average, to leave out penny stocks and thinly traded shares.
  • The same stock is counted at most once every 10 trading days, so one collapse isn't counted several times.
  • "Back to the pre-crash price" means any daily close at or above the close the day before the crash, within 20, 60, 125 or 250 trading days (about 1, 3, 6 and 12 months). Each percentage only counts crashes old enough to have had that full window.
  • Prices are adjusted for splits and dividends.
  • The data covers stocks that are still listed today. Companies that crashed and were later delisted, acquired or went bankrupt aren't included, and many of those never recovered, so the true recovery rates are probably lower than shown.
